About this reaction time test

This reaction time test measures how quickly you respond to a visual signal — the classic "click when it turns green" benchmark. After a random delay of 1.2 to 4 seconds the box switches from red to green, and the tool records the milliseconds between that switch and your click using the browser's high-resolution performance.now() timer. It runs five attempts and reports your average, your best single attempt, and a qualitative band so you can see how fast your reflexes are compared with typical human numbers.

Simple visual reaction time for adults averages around 273 ms, with trained gamers and athletes often landing in the 180–220 ms range and the theoretical human floor sitting near 150 ms. Reaction time is a useful proxy for alertness and focus, which is why it is used in sleep research, sports training, and driver-safety studies. It works on desktop with a mouse and on phones and tablets with touch, needs no signup, and runs entirely in your browser.

How to use this reaction time test

1. Start the test

Click or tap the box. It turns red and shows "Wait for green…". Keep your cursor or finger ready.

2. Wait for green

After a random 1.2–4 second delay the box turns green. If you click while it is still red, the attempt restarts.

3. Click instantly

The moment it turns green, click. The tool records your time in milliseconds and moves to the next attempt.

4. Read your score

After 5 attempts you get your average, your best attempt, and a band showing how your reflexes compare. Hit "Try again" to retry.

Frequently asked questions

For a simple visual reaction time test like this one, anything from roughly 200 to 270 ms is considered good for an adult. The average human sits around 273 ms. Times under 200 ms are excellent and typical of trained gamers or athletes, while 300 ms and above is still perfectly normal, especially on a laptop trackpad or with display and input lag in the mix.
Reaction time is fastest in the late teens and twenties, usually around 250 to 270 ms on a click test, then slows gradually. Many people are near 280 to 300 ms in their forties and 300 to 350 ms past age sixty. Children are slower than young adults because their attention and motor control are still developing. These are averages only; individual variation and practice matter more than age alone.
Practice on the specific task helps most, so repeat the test and warm up before measuring. Beyond that, get enough sleep, stay hydrated, limit alcohol, use a wired mouse and a high-refresh-rate monitor, and remove distractions. Regular aerobic exercise and fast-paced sports or games also produce small but real improvements. Do not expect dramatic gains; shaving 20 to 40 ms off your average is a realistic target.
Common causes are fatigue, low focus, caffeine wearing off, and simply not being warmed up. Hardware matters too: a wireless mouse, a 60 Hz screen, browser background load, and touchscreen input can each add 10 to 50 ms. Anticipating the green signal and clicking early also inflates your average because those attempts restart. Run several clean rounds when you are alert to see your true baseline.
Yes. A 200 ms average on a simple visual reaction test is faster than most people and is in the range associated with competitive gamers and athletes. The theoretical floor for human visual reaction is around 150 ms because of the time light takes to be processed by the retina, travel through the nervous system, and trigger a muscle response, so consistently hitting 200 ms is close to optimal.
